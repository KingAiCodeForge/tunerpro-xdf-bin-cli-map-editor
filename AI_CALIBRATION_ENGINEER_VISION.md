# KingAI AI Calibration Engineer

**KingAI Pty Ltd | Founder vision | 26 October 2026**

**Status:** This is the direction I want to take the project, not a claim that autonomous ECU tuning, datalog correction or ECU flashing is already implemented.

I have been thinking about how much of ECU tuning is really a human operating a feedback loop. A tuner connects to the ECU, collects logs, looks at AFR, fuel trims, ignition timing, knock retard, load and other engine conditions, makes calibration adjustments, tests them and repeats the process. In other words, they observe, analyse, decide, correct and verify. I think AI could eventually take over much of the repetitive analysis and calculation involved in that workflow, while also teaching someone what is happening and why.

What I want to build through KingAI is an AI calibration engineer that works for someone learning to tune and for someone who has been tuning engines for years. I do not want a chatbot that simply guesses which fuel table to change or suggests an arbitrary correction percentage. I want a system that understands the exact ECU software being worked on, identifies relevant calibration controls from verified evidence, interprets actual measurements and can explain the reasoning behind every proposed adjustment.

**Teach Me** is how I imagine someone getting started. They could ask the AI to teach them how to calibrate a MAF sensor using their own vehicle rather than a generic example. The AI would explain what the sensors measure, how short and long term fuel trims relate to commanded fueling, what data is missing, and why a particular table may be relevant. It could display the current values, show the calculations, highlight the operating regions covered by a log and walk through the proposed correction. The person would be learning to tune while the software does the repetitive analysis.

**Help Me** is for a tuner who already understands the process. They could provide their exact BIN and XDF along with compatible logging definitions and a datalog, then ask the AI to find the operating regions that need attention. The system could organise the measurements, show what evidence supports each suspected problem, propose changes to identified cells and produce a reviewable calibration diff. I want the tuner to see the expected encoded values and every changed byte, not just a message claiming the tune has been improved. Where the evidence is weak, the AI should identify the missing information instead of filling in the gaps.

**Analyse and Optimise** is the longer term closed loop idea. A log from calibration A would be linked to the exact file and vehicle configuration used for that session. The AI would analyse it, propose a bounded adjustment, and the editor would create a new calibration B with its own hash and audit record. After a separate, controlled test, the next log could be compared with the previous one to see whether the intended result actually occurred. Over several sessions I want the system to preserve the history of what changed, why it changed, what was measured and what did not work. That history could make later decisions better informed without pretending that one good looking graph proves a tune is correct.

The existing KingAI CLI Map Editor gives me part of the foundation. On the current public release it can inspect compatible XDF and BIN inputs, run structural preflight checks, display supported tables and scalars, compare binaries, export data and perform a limited set of guarded edits to new files with audit evidence. It is deliberately a deterministic file tool that a coding agent can operate. It does not currently decode and correlate ADX/XDL datalogs, calculate automatic corrections, communicate with an ECU or flash a calibration. An ADX describes logging channels and conversions, while an XDL is a captured log; neither is itself a flashing interface.

The separation between the AI and the editor matters to me. I want the AI to interpret the measurements, teach the reasoning and propose engineering values. I want tested software to handle file identity, conversion equations, supported storage formats, rounding, source checks, conflict detection, output verification and exact byte differences. A logged operating point also has to be associated with the right calibration axes and source image before it can justify a cell proposal. If that relationship has not been established, the AI should say so. The correction should never be based only on a familiar table name or a coincidental correlation in a log.

The difficult part is teaching the system when not to make a correction. A lean AFR reading might reflect a calibration problem, but it might also involve incorrect sensor calibration, fuel pressure, injector data, an air leak or a transient operating condition. A mathematically precise change can still be completely wrong for the engine. I want the AI to look at measurement quality, operating conditions, sample coverage, unit conversions, mechanical context and uncertainty before it recommends anything. Sometimes the right answer should be to request another log, inspect the hardware or stop the calibration experiment.

I would build this in stages. First comes reliable read only ingestion of exported logs, with each session bound to its actual BIN, XDF and applicable ADX identity. Next comes verified channel interpretation, operating cell coverage and a clear teaching and analysis interface. Then comes a declarative edit proposal that the CLI can preview, validate, execute within its supported scope and audit. Comparison across later sessions would follow. Checksum handling, hardware adapters and any physical ECU writing are separate engineering projects that need exact platform evidence, independent verification and controlled testing. A successful file edit is not proof that an ECU image is flash ready, and interrupted ECU programming cannot be assumed to support automatic rollback.

The bigger opportunity is that the same system could be useful in very different ways. A beginner could say, “Teach me why my fuel trims are positive and calculate an example correction for review.” An experienced tuner could say, “Compare these logs with my last calibration, identify the supported changes and give me the complete diff.” Both would use the same evidence and the same engineering tools, with different levels of explanation. That is what I want KingAI to become: software that can do much of the engineering work, show its reasoning and recognise when there is not enough information to make a reliable decision.
